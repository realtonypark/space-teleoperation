Arms in Orbit

Can people on Earth teleoperate simple robot arms inside a rideshare-class satellite well enough to collect physical-AI demonstrations that no Earth-side simulation or lab can produce? A research fleet answered with literature, a socket-level testbed, 20 hypotheses, 9,700 emulated episodes, two adversarial audits and an independent reproduction.

What the numbers say

The satellite never runs a model. It plays a 50 Hz stream of joint setpoints through a 30 ms buffer, holds after 300 ms of silence and stows after 10 s. On the relay link that baseline already meets the acceptance criterion for both tasks. Latency only starts to bite past the relay: coarse capture of a drifting box degrades from about 250 ms round trip, and 6 mm-clearance peg insertion from about 100 ms.

  • Link. Two Starlink mini-laser terminals as primary; a four-site polar network as fallback and commissioning path; L-band as the safety channel. GEO relay is disqualified by its 474 ms propagation floor.
  • Protocol. Timestamped, sequence-keyed joint setpoints at 50 Hz, sent twice, played out through a fixed 30 ms buffer. Loss on the relay (1.1 %) is irrelevant to a self-healing setpoint stream; three transport hypotheses abstained after computing exactly that.
  • Satellite. Decision (c): hold, retract, ramp, static envelopes and hardware cutoffs on a deterministic supervisor. No vision or models in the baseline. The onboard terminal primitive is the one addition the evidence justifies, and it costs a Jetson pose estimator.
  • Ground. Leader-follower or VR without force feedback, one write authority per arm, the twin view as the default operator display, chained-release task design to remove resets.
  • Mission. Pressurised ESPA-class bus (Apex Aries class, 100 kg), a 100 to 300 L aluminium cylinder at 1 atm, four Dynamixel-class arms with granular and liquid cells, order of $6 to 12 M including launch. A 16U pass-only variant carries two arms and no relay.

What the data buys

Four tasks expose behaviour no simulator validates and no Earth analog sustains beyond 24 s of free fall: scooping and pouring sieved granular simulant, capturing a truly free 6-DoF tumbling body, capillary pours between vessels, and routing cable and fabric in microgravity. All four are slow by nature, which is the largest latency hider available, and all four fit a 6-DoF arm with a parallel gripper inside a pressurised cell.

What it does not prove. Video is modelled as delay plus bytes, not a codec. The grasp is kinematic once the physical jaw closes on the box. The operator is a synthetic pursuit controller with a 170 ms reaction delay, not a person. Everything runs in real time on one machine. No third-party satellite has published a latency over Starlink lasers, no radiation data exists for the hobby servos, and no policy has been trained on latency-collected demonstrations to see whether it is worse. Those are the flight questions.
